SCREEN 04B Approval Queue at Volume
Open decision 01 · resolved

At five a day it's a list. At twenty it's an inbox. Inboxes kill executive products.

The founder didn't hire an AI executive to get another queue. So the design goal here is inverted from every inbox ever built: success is the queue shrinking, and the metric on the wall is not throughput — it's the share of decisions Kalpavriksha stops needing to ask about.

The failure mode, stated plainly. A red badge with "23" on it turns an executive partner back into a to-do app. The founder starts triaging the AI instead of the business. Every decision below is defence against that one outcome.

Principle 01

Rank by consequence, never by time

A chronological queue makes the founder do the prioritisation the AI should have done. Kalpavriksha ranks by irreversibility × exposure × deadline and says so. Time is metadata, not order.

Principle 02

Delegating judgment beats delegating tasks

Batching is a bandage. The cure is a standing rule — the founder teaching the AI a decision boundary once, and never seeing that class of request again. Rules are the product.

Principle 03

Silence must have a stated default

Every item declares what happens if the founder never responds. "If I don't hear from you by Thursday 18:00, I'll let it renew." Pre-committed defaults are what make a queue survivable — ignoring it becomes a decision, not a debt.

Aggregate exposure is always visible before commit. Approving nine things one at a time hides the total; approving them together must not. The running rupee figure is the single guardrail that makes batching safe.

Also available per item
Snooze

Defer with a re-surface time. "Bring this back Monday." The item leaves the queue entirely — no ghost, no badge.

Mute

Silence a whole class for a period. "Nothing about tooling spend until Q4." Kalpavriksha still logs what it did — muting hides the ask, never the receipt.

Delegate

Route to a human. Invoices to the CFO, seats to the Head of Eng. The most under-designed option in every approval product — a founder at 20/day usually shouldn't be the approver at all.

Standing rules

The AI proposes the rule. The founder never writes one.

A settings page for approval rules is where rules go to die — nobody authors policy preemptively. So Kalpavriksha watches its own rejection history and asks for the boundary once it has earned the right to.

This is the emotional peak of the whole product. Not the neural tree. The moment the AI says "you've said yes nine times out of nine — want me to stop asking?" is when the founder feels genuinely accompanied rather than assisted.

Rules expire. Every rule carries a review date and dies unless renewed. A permission granted in month one shouldn't still be running silently in year two — expiry is what keeps the founder's mental model of "what can it do?" accurate.

Trust anchors

Autonomy is only comfortable if it's instantly revocable.

This answers open decision 04 too. "AUTONOMY · LEVEL 3" on Mission Control becomes real here: level is derived from active rules, and the founder can collapse it to zero in one gesture.

Pause all autonomy

Every rule stops firing immediately. Nothing already committed is undone. Kalpavriksha keeps working and keeps queueing — it just stops deciding.

Deliberately refused

What this queue will never have

✕
Unread badges and counts. A number on a nav item is an obligation. Kalpavriksha reports "2 need you," never "23 unread."
✕
Inbox-zero gamification. No streaks, no confetti, no "all caught up!" The goal is fewer asks, not a cleared list.
✕
Bulk-approve on tier 1. Irreversible decisions never get a select-all, at any volume.
✕
Silent rule creation. The AI proposes; the founder grants. No rule ever activates from inferred consent.
✕
A settings page called "Approval Rules." Rules live where decisions live — proposed in context, reviewed in context.
Where this lands in the product

Same screen, different density

Screen 04 doesn't fork. Below roughly five open items the Approval Center renders as it does today — full consequence cards, no checkboxes, no tiers. Past that threshold it progressively densifies: tiers appear, the sweep list collapses to rows, the commit bar docks.

The founder never learns two interfaces, and the transition itself is information: the day the tiers appear is the day the founder learns they're becoming the bottleneck.
